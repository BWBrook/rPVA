## What sensitivity analysis asks

A model conclusion can depend strongly on an uncertain assumption. **One-factor
sensitivity analysis** changes one assumption at a time, reruns the same future,
and asks how much the outcomes respond.

This is useful because it helps you identify assumptions that deserve careful
measurement, justification, or management attention. It does not show how two
or more assumptions might interact, and it does not prove which value is true.

## The assumptions you can test

Choose one of eight model factors: breeding-territory capacity, all-stage
survival, juvenile survival, fecundity, annual catastrophe probability,
catastrophe survival, environmental variability, or habitat-capacity
variability. The page shows the factor's biological meaning, units, reviewed
bounds and interpretation boundary.

Each comparison retains the current no-change value and adds one numerically
higher and one lower alternative, so the baseline is bracketed rather than
tested in only one direction. The displayed ranges are deliberately generous:
they support exploration and can include values that are unlikely in the real
system. The alternatives and bounds are teaching assumptions, not empirical
estimates or forecasts.
For example, the breeding-territory comparison retains the common-future value
of 325 territories.

Some factors have deliberately narrow meanings. Juvenile survival changes
stages 1 and 2 only. Catastrophe probability changes event frequency, whereas
catastrophe survival changes event severity. Environmental variability changes
annual survival and fecundity draws without imposing one shared good or bad
year. Habitat-capacity variability changes variation around the mean, not the
mean capacity itself.

## Why the comparison is fair

All three settings begin with the same 2025 population and use the same age
structure, 75-year horizon, and 1,000 simulated futures. Only the selected
factor changes; every other model setting remains at the common-future value.
That isolation lets you attribute differences among the three model results to
the factor being tested.

## How to read the results

The abundance panel shows the median number of birds in 2100 and the middle 95%
of simulated outcomes. Wide intervals indicate that chance still produces a
range of possible futures within a single setting.

The risk panel shows the proportion of futures that fell **strictly below 100
territory-holding breeding pairs** at least once between 2025 and 2100. This
working warning threshold is approximately the population scale that preceded
rodent eradication. Historical survey totals count birds, whereas this model
metric uses a territory-holder proxy for breeding pairs, so it is not a direct
conversion of a past census. It is also not a universal minimum viable
population or a proven biological tipping point.

Complete extinction is still reported in the comparison table. It is usually
rare under these bounded scenarios, so the main risk plot emphasizes the more
informative 100-pair warning event.

If only some futures cross the threshold, the timing summary describes those
crossing futures only. It should not be interpreted as the expected timing for
every future.

## What to record

Before running, you may record a question and prediction. Afterwards, separate:

- the **pattern** supported by the comparison; and
- a **limitation**, such as uncertainty in the chosen setting, an omitted
  ecological process, or the fact that one-factor analysis does not test
  interactions.

These notes are evidence for your own reasoning. They do not need to follow a
fixed wording or reproduce an answer supplied by the app.
